# Plan a Printed QR Enquiry Journey for Independent Consultants

A practical walkthrough for guiding a prospective client from a printed QR code to a focused, mobile-friendly project enquiry page.

OrbitPage Editorial · 2026-11-20 · English

[Read on OrbitPage](<https://orbitpage.com/en-US/blog/printed-qr-enquiry-journey-consultants>) · [Browse the archive](<https://github.com/paoloronco/OrbitPage/tree/main/docs/blog>)

![From conference handout to useful enquiry](<https://orbitpage.com/api/blog/illustrations/printed-qr-enquiry-journey-consultants?v=2>)

A QR code can scan perfectly and still leave a prospective client unsure what to do next. For an independent consultant, the print, the page and the enquiry route need to work as one short journey.

<a id="section-1"></a>

## Start with the moment of the scan

Decide where a likely prospect will encounter the code: on a conference handout, a proposal, a presentation slide or a printed introduction. That setting shapes what they need to see first. Someone who has just heard you describe a service needs a quick way to qualify fit; someone reading a leave-behind may need more context.

Place the code beside a specific invitation, not as an unexplained graphic. For example: “Planning a service redesign? Scan to share your goals and timeline.” That copy sets an expectation and helps a reader decide whether scanning is relevant.

Give the code clear space and keep it off folds, glossy glare and places likely to be covered by a hand or label. Include a short, readable fallback address where the layout allows, so the next step does not depend entirely on a camera.

<a id="section-2"></a>

## Choose a destination that can stay current

Point the code to a page you control and expect to keep useful, rather than directly to a temporary campaign or booking link. A printed code is hard to replace once distributed; a stable page lets you revise its wording or enquiry route without reprinting, provided the public address itself remains available.

OrbitPage pages can be shared at a public workspace address or an active custom domain. If you use a custom domain, wait until its ownership, routing and HTTPS are ready before printing the address. Save page edits before opening the public page: an unsaved preview does not change what visitors see.

Make the page’s main action clear, but do not force every visitor into a booking flow. A booking provider is an external destination, while the OrbitPage page can explain your service and direct visitors to that provider or another enquiry route.

## Build for a visitor holding a phone

Assume the reader is standing, distracted and using a small screen. Put the service and intended client near the top, followed by a short explanation of the project types you take on and a clear next step. Keep secondary material below the primary action rather than making visitors search through a long list of links.

A qualification prompt can make an enquiry more useful without turning the page into a full proposal form. For example, invite the visitor to include the project goal, current stage, desired timing and how to contact them. If you direct them to an external form or booking tool, state that plainly so the transition is expected.

There is a real tradeoff between gathering enough detail and making contact easy. More questions can help you assess fit, but may discourage someone who is ready to start a conversation. Ask only for information that will change your next response.

<a id="section-4"></a>

## Hypothetical worked example: a conference handout

Fictional consultant Maya advises small teams on service redesign. Her handout reaches people immediately after a short conference conversation, so her QR destination should help them check fit and send a useful first enquiry, rather than open a general biography.

Beside the code, the handout reads: “Considering a service redesign? Scan to check fit and send a project note.” At the top of the page, Maya writes: “I help small teams make confusing services easier to use. Tell me what is not working and where you want to get.” A short paragraph names the kinds of projects she takes on, followed by an enquiry action labelled “Share your project context.”

The page asks for a project goal, current stage and preferred timing, with an optional way to provide contact details. If Maya uses an external form, she checks that its title and first question match the handout’s promise. She keeps the QR destination at her stable public page, so she can update the enquiry route later without changing the printed code.

<a id="section-5"></a>

## Approve the complete print-to-page journey

Before sending artwork to print, test the journey as a new visitor rather than relying on a preview or on the fact that the code has been generated. Use the final artwork at its intended size, scan it from a phone in the setting where it will be used, and follow the route all the way to its next action. This is a manual check; the QR code itself does not confirm that the page or an external form is current.

Make the last review an approval gate: compare the promise printed beside the code with what the page and enquiry route actually deliver, then decide whether the proof is ready to distribute.

-   Confirm the code resolves to the intended public address, not an editor or preview.
-   Check that the fallback address is legible and matches the printed destination.
-   Verify the enquiry route is live and asks only for useful project details.
-   Approve the proof only when the page and print invitation make the same promise.
