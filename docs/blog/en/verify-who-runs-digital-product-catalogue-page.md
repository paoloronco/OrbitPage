# How Digital Product Makers Can Show Who Runs a Catalogue Page

A practical walkthrough for making a small digital product catalogue easier to verify, from naming its publisher to checking contact routes and destinations.

OrbitPage Editorial · 2026-11-20 · English

[Read on OrbitPage](<https://orbitpage.com/en-US/blog/verify-who-runs-digital-product-catalogue-page>) · [Browse the archive](<https://github.com/paoloronco/OrbitPage/tree/main/docs/blog>)

![Manually verify the Fieldnote example](<https://orbitpage.com/api/blog/illustrations/verify-who-runs-digital-product-catalogue-page?v=2>)

A catalogue can look polished and still leave a first-time visitor asking basic questions: Who made these files? Is this the current page? Where does a purchase or support question go? Make those answers visible before asking someone to trust a product link. The checks below help a maker explain the person or small business behind a digital catalogue without implying that a page automatically verifies identity.

<a id="section-1"></a>

## Begin with a name that visitors can reconcile

Use the same recognizable publisher name in the page heading, product descriptions, and the destinations visitors reach from the catalogue. If you sell as a studio but publish under your own name, explain that relationship in one plain sentence. A visitor should not have to infer that two different names belong together.

Give the page a concise description of what the catalogue contains and who maintains it. Specificity helps a visitor compare the page with a social post, an email, or a product file they already encountered. It is context, not proof of legal identity.

For example, copy might read: “Fieldnote Studio is run by Mara Chen. This catalogue contains printable planning templates and short guides created by the studio.” Only use a name and claim you can stand behind. If you do not publish a personal name, use the business or project name consistently and state that it is the publisher.

-   Match the publisher name across the page and product destinations.
-   Explain any difference between a personal name and studio name.
-   Describe what is in the catalogue without making unsupported claims.

<a id="section-2"></a>

## Make contact routes specific and usable

A contact label should tell visitors what happens when they use it. “Contact” is less informative than “Email the maker about a file or order.” If you provide a support address, say what kinds of questions belong there and how a visitor should identify the relevant product.

Avoid displaying a personal phone number or home address merely to appear credible. Choose a contact route you can monitor, and check it manually from a visitor’s point of view. A visible address is not useful if it is misspelled, abandoned, or tied to an account visitors cannot access.

If you use a contact form or an external social profile, identify it as the route it is. Do not imply that the page itself provides a staffed help desk or a response timetable unless you actually maintain one. A modest, accurate expectation is better than a promise you may not keep.

-   Name the purpose of each contact route.
-   Check that the address or destination is typed correctly.
-   State response expectations only if you can consistently meet them.

## Explain the product before sending visitors elsewhere

A product grid rarely answers why an item is useful or what a buyer will receive. Introduce the catalogue with a short explanation, then make each product destination understandable before the visitor leaves your page. OrbitPage can bring routes together on a public page, while a clear introduction gives those routes context.

For each item, state the format, intended use, and any important limitation in direct language. For a downloadable planner, that could mean identifying the file types and whether the pages are designed for printing or screen use. Explain usage terms and support details where relevant. Do not rely on a thumbnail or title to communicate those conditions.

When a purchase uses an external destination, label that destination plainly, such as “View the workbook and purchase details at \[provider name\].” Replace the bracketed text with the actual provider name; do not leave a placeholder on the published page. Check that the external destination describes the same item, seller, format, terms, and currency as your catalogue.

-   Say what file or service the visitor should expect.
-   Describe format, usage terms, and support where relevant.
-   Label external purchase destinations instead of implying they are native.

<a id="section-4"></a>

## Hypothetical worked example: Fieldnote Studio

This example is fictional. Imagine that Mara Chen publishes a small catalogue of printable planning tools under the name Fieldnote Studio. A visitor arrives from a post that shows one planner but does not identify the maker or explain what the catalogue includes.

At the top of the page, Mara could write: “Fieldnote Studio is a small catalogue of printable planning tools by Mara Chen. Browse the formats and usage notes below, or contact the studio with a question about a specific item.” That statement links the studio name to a named maker and sets the scope without claiming independent verification.

A product description might say: “Weekly Project Map — PDF download for planning one project across a week. Review the included pages and personal-use terms on the purchase page before ordering.” The exact wording should match the actual file and terms. If the item is delivered through an external shop, the button could read: “See file details and purchase options at \[actual shop name\].” The destination must be checked by Mara, not assumed to be correct because the button looks right.

For support, she might display: “Questions about a Fieldnote Studio file? Email \[fictional address: help@example.invalid\] and include the product name.” This address is explicitly fictional and must not be published as a real contact route. In a real catalogue, Mara would replace it with an address she controls and test it herself.

-   Keep the publisher identity visible beside the catalogue.
-   Describe the deliverable and terms before the purchase handoff.
-   Replace all fictional names and addresses with verified details before publishing.

<a id="section-5"></a>

## Check every destination and the delivered item

Treat each button as a route a visitor must be able to understand and complete. Open the public page in a fresh browser session, follow each route, and compare the destination with the text that introduced it. This is a manual check; do not assume that a label or link automatically confirms the destination’s owner.

For an OrbitPage Shop item, review the actual protected file or service instructions, then compare the offer’s formats, usage terms, price, currency, and support details with what the public page says. Keep paid files in the private Shop file store rather than linking their contents from public uploads. If the purchase happens elsewhere, inspect that provider’s page and delivery instructions separately.

Also check the routes that may still be circulating outside the catalogue. If you use a custom domain, review discovery and sitemap URLs, provider domain settings, newsletter links, and printed QR destinations after a domain change. A printed QR code continues to point to the address encoded when it was made; changing the public hostname does not rewrite the code.

-   Open every link and confirm its destination matches its label.
-   Compare the offer with the delivered file or service instructions.
-   Review old newsletter links and printed QR codes after changing a domain.
-   Remove or correct any route that leads to an outdated or mismatched offer.

<a id="section-6"></a>

## Do a visitor-side identity check before sharing

A page can make its publisher easier to understand, but it cannot by itself establish that every identity claim is true. Consider whether a cautious stranger can connect the page name, maker or business description, contact route, and purchase destination without guessing. Where those details differ, explain why.

There is a tradeoff between giving enough context and exposing unnecessary personal information. A maker can identify a studio and a controlled support address without publishing a home address. Likewise, one central public destination can frame a catalogue without requiring a full portfolio rebuild, but it should not pretend to replace every external checkout or support system.

The next action is concrete: open the page as a visitor and work through the checklist below. Fix the first mismatch you find, then repeat the walk-through before sharing the catalogue.

-   Can a visitor tell who publishes the catalogue and what it contains?
-   Does each contact route say what it is for and reach an account you control?
-   Do product terms, formats, and destinations agree with the actual offer?
-   Can a visitor distinguish the public catalogue from an external purchase or support service?
