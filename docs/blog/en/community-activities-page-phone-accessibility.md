# Make a Community Activities Page Easier to Use on a Phone

A focused walkthrough for simplifying a recurring-activities page, clarifying links, adding useful image descriptions, and checking accessibility on a phone.

OrbitPage Editorial · 2026-11-26 · English

[Read on OrbitPage](<https://orbitpage.com/en-US/blog/community-activities-page-phone-accessibility>) · [Browse the archive](<https://github.com/paoloronco/OrbitPage/tree/main/docs/blog>)

![Turn a monthly notice into a clear next step](<https://orbitpage.com/api/blog/illustrations/community-activities-page-phone-accessibility?v=2>)

When a community page puts several activities, dates and sign-up links together, visitors on phones can struggle to find what is current or tap the right action. A compact page works better when its order, labels and controls make the next step obvious.

<a id="section-1"></a>

## Put the next useful action first

Start by deciding what most visitors need to do: find the next gathering, book a place, or get directions. Arrange the page around that need, rather than giving every activity equal weight. A short introduction can identify the group and say what the page helps visitors do.

Use a clear heading for each activity, with its date and location close by. A link is enough when its label and destination explain the action; use an event-style block when timing and booking context belong together. OrbitPage supports choosing content blocks to suit the information, so you can keep the page focused instead of adding a block for every detail.

<a id="section-2"></a>

## Replace vague labels with useful action copy

A label such as “Click here” or “More” does not tell someone where a link goes, especially when links are read out of context. Name the destination or action directly. Keep the label short enough to scan, but specific enough to distinguish it from other links.

For a recurring activity, try “View the Saturday garden session details” or “Book a place for the repair café.” If the booking form is on another service, make that clear in the surrounding copy: “Registration opens on the external booking form.” OrbitPage can display a link to that destination; the external service handles the booking itself.

Before and after: change “Join us” to “Register for the 12 May community walk.” Change “Find us” to “Get directions to the library meeting room.” These labels tell visitors both what happens and where the action leads.

## Hypothetical example: simplify a monthly notice

Imagine the fictional Riverside Neighbours group has a compact page with a monthly litter-pick, a shared tools session and a contact link. The current page starts with a general welcome, puts several “Details” links together, and uses a photo of volunteers without describing what it shows.

Reorder it so the next litter-pick appears first. Use the heading “Next litter-pick: Saturday 18 May,” followed by “Meet at the east gate of Mill Park at 10:00.” Label the destination “Register for the 18 May litter-pick” if it opens a booking form, or “Read the litter-pick details” if it opens an information page. Put the shared tools session below, then a direct contact route.

For a photo that adds useful context, write alternative text such as “Volunteers collecting litter beside the park path.” If the image is only decorative and adds no information, do not make its alt text repeat the nearby activity heading. Alternative text should communicate the image’s relevant purpose, not describe every visual detail.

<a id="section-4"></a>

## Keep the page compact without hiding decisions

Removing clutter can make a page easier to scan, but excessive compression can leave visitors guessing. Do not omit a date, meeting point or booking requirement just to save space. Keep those details next to the activity they belong to, and remove repeated introductions or links that lead to the same place.

A useful tradeoff is whether to show every recurring activity at once or prioritize the next one. Showing the full schedule helps visitors plan ahead; placing the next date first helps someone looking for an immediate action. A brief “Other regular activities” section can preserve the wider schedule without competing with the next event.

<a id="section-5"></a>

## Check the page by hand on a phone

A preview is helpful, but it does not replace using the published page. Open it on a phone or narrow browser window, increase text size if possible, and navigate without relying on a mouse. Check that headings make sense in order, links can be reached and activated by keyboard, and focus remains visible. Try zooming in to see whether controls still fit without forcing sideways scrolling.

Use this final checklist before sharing the page. It covers the main reading and operating tasks, rather than assuming a layout or editor setting has checked accessibility for you.

-   Can a visitor identify the next activity, date and meeting place at a glance?
-   Do link labels explain their action or destination, including external booking forms?
-   Does each informative image have concise, relevant alternative text?
-   Can you reach every control by keyboard and see which one has focus?
-   At narrow width and larger text, can you use the page without sideways scrolling?
