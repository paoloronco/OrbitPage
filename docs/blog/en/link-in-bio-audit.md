# A 15-minute audit for a link-in-bio page that stays useful

Audit a link-in-bio page in 15 minutes: check the public URL, first screen, links, expired claims and the action that matters.

OrbitPage · 2026-09-17 · English

[Read on OrbitPage](<https://orbitpage.com/en-US/blog/link-in-bio-audit>) · [Browse the archive](<https://github.com/paoloronco/OrbitPage/tree/main/docs/blog>)

A page can look finished even when its most important link no longer works. A short recurring audit is often more valuable than a redesign: it catches stale dates, broken links and confusing priorities before visitors have to report them. Use the live public page, not just the editor preview.

<a id="open"></a>

## Open the public URL as a stranger would.

Use a private browser window and a phone. Check that the page loads, its name and first sentence identify the owner, and the primary action is visible without searching. If the social profile promises a specific event or offer, look for it on the first screen.

Check the browser title and preview description against the visible page. Fix a mismatch before spending time on colors or extra blocks.

<a id="follow"></a>

## Follow every important path to its destination.

Open the primary action, then the booking, menu, Shop, download, email and social links that matter. Look for errors, expired pages, wrong accounts, old prices and links that require an unexpected login.

Prioritize by consequence: a broken booking route or misleading price comes before a low-traffic archive link. Remove a dead destination when there is no current replacement rather than leaving an attractive button that fails.

<a id="freshness"></a>

## Check every time-sensitive promise.

Read dates, availability, opening hours, campaign language and featured products as if today were the visitor's first encounter. Replace or archive finished promotions, and confirm that the next event is actually the next one.

Ask who owns each update. A weekly page check can be small because it is a routine; a page with no owner slowly becomes a collection of stale claims.

<a id="decision"></a>

## Choose one fix and record what changed.

If visitors reach the page but ignore the main action, test a clearer label or order. If they click but do not complete the job, inspect the destination. Use visits and action clicks as clues, then confirm bookings, enquiries or sales in the system that owns them.

Write down the date, the problem and the one change made. At the next audit, compare the same visitor task. This keeps the page improving without turning every small dip into a redesign.
